# Solar Physics Lab: Reconstructing Orbital Mechanics in the Browser

<Callout type="info" title="The Core Thesis">
Abstract celestial mechanics are notoriously difficult for students to grasp because textbook diagrams reduce 3D rotational geometry to flat 2D projections. 

By grounding <strong>spherical trigonometry in real-time WebGL rendering</strong> and pairing it with <strong>context-aware AI analysts</strong>, we can transform abstract vectors into direct human intuition.
</Callout>

/>

<div className="p-4 border-l-4 border-black dark:border-venom-slime bg-black/5 dark:bg-white/5 font-serif text-lg sm:text-xl font-medium leading-relaxed my-8">
  Celestial mechanics is fundamentally about perspective, rotation, and time.
</div>

When students learn about the seasons, solar declination, or why winter days are short, they are typically shown static 2D diagrams with curved arrows. 

These 2D representations force the human brain to mentally rotate multiple reference frames at once: the heliocentric orbital plane, the Earth's tilted rotational axis, and the local horizon of a standing observer.

Solar Physics Lab was designed to eliminate this cognitive overhead. 

By building a physically consistent 3D simulation in the browser, users can manipulate orbital variables and instantly witness the geometric consequences on the ground.

<SectionBanner 
  act="MATHEMATICS" 
  number="03" 
  title="The Spherical Trigonometry Engine" 
  subtitle="How orbital coordinates translate into local azimuth and altitude" 
  color="emerald" 
/>

<div className="p-4 border-l-4 border-black dark:border-venom-slime bg-black/5 dark:bg-white/5 font-serif text-lg sm:text-xl font-medium leading-relaxed my-8">
  The simulation is governed by mathematical laws, not interpolated keyframes.
</div>

The position of the Sun in the sky is computed using exact coordinate transformations from celestial latitude ($\phi$), solar declination ($\delta$), and hour angle ($\omega$):

$$
x = \cos(\delta)\sin(\omega)
$$

$$
y = \sin(\delta)
$$

$$
z = \cos(\delta)\cos(\omega)
$$

From these vectors, the local observer frame constructs an orthonormal coordinate basis (East, Zenith, North), calculating the exact solar elevation angle ($\alpha$) and shadow length ($L = h/\tan(\alpha)$) anywhere on Earth.
